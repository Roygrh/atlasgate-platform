## 1. Framing and comparison questions

1. **Daniel Okafor:** We need to ask which steps are truly shared, which differ because of business context, which differences are accidental versus necessary, where ownership transfers or only appears to, and what evidence must exist before requirements.
2. **Priya Raman:** A shared concern is not a shared sequence. Common context, traceability, and visibility might support different workflows without making one universal process.
3. **Aisha Bello:** Commerce owns Commerce behavior and partner commitments. Platform support can coordinate shared concerns, but it does not gain authority over pricing, order, tenant, or partner meaning.
4. **Lucas Pereira:** Loyalty similarly owns balance, eligibility, and reward semantics. A platform cannot resolve a consumer's interpretation merely because it carries or documents an API.
5. **Jordan Lee:** Platform Engineering can explain supported environments and shared-service constraints. Being the reachable coordinator can look like an ownership transfer even when domain authority remains where it was.
6. **Marcus Reed:** SES-003 and SES-004 already leave review evidence, safe support access, criticality, telemetry, and consequence authority unresolved. We can identify where those questions enter, not invent controls or service targets.

## 2. Scenario one - Commerce partner launch change

7. **Aisha Bello:** A partner launch begins with a dated commercial trigger. Commerce clarifies behavior and partner treatment, then coordinates reviewers, platform support, partner testing, release, and support readiness; the partner controls part of readiness.
8. **Daniel Okafor:** Does Commerce hand responsibility to the partner when specifications are sent?
9. **Aisha Bello:** Coordination moves, not business authority. Commerce still owns its behavior and explanation; the partner owns its implementation and evidence of readiness. Neither can complete the other's work.
10. **Jordan Lee:** Access or environment requests often arrive after commercial discussion. Platform can process supported context, but missing owner, environment, purpose, or review evidence sends work backward and hides waiting.
11. **Marcus Reed:** Production criticality can vary by launch and period. Deployment completion is not enough: partner transactions, support readiness, and an agreed completion interpretation may still be outstanding.
12. **Aisha Bello:** For one recalled launch, partner confirmation and review coordination may have added roughly seven to ten business days. That estimate is explicitly unverified and combines external waiting with Northstar activity.
13. **Priya Raman:** A universal gate would misattribute that mixed delay. We need timestamps, returned inputs, decision provenance, partner pauses, and operational consequences before proposing anything.

## 3. Scenario two - Commerce tenant-specific policy change

14. **Aisha Bello:** An urgent tenant-specific request can seek different traffic or business treatment. Commerce must interpret the business request; the platform cannot decide whether the exception is commercially acceptable.
15. **Jordan Lee:** Teams sometimes copy a previous tenant's treatment because it is locally fast. That shifts risk into assumption drift, cleanup, support, and uncertainty about who approved the difference.
16. **Marcus Reed:** Urgency also creates recovery questions: who recognizes harm, who can reverse domain behavior, and what verifies restoration? SES-004 did not grant the platform consequence-acceptance authority.
17. **Priya Raman:** We must not turn a domain-specific urgent exception practice into a global standard. We also must not assume every difference is necessary merely because it exists today.
18. **Daniel Okafor:** So the open distinction is between necessary tenant/business variation and avoidable inconsistency in intake, context, review timing, or ownership visibility.
19. **Aisha Bello:** Yes, and security review remains a dependency where applicable under SES-003 evidence questions. A launch date coordinates people; it does not authorize an exception or select a policy mechanism.

## 4. Scenario three - Loyalty semantic API change

20. **Lucas Pereira:** A balance, eligibility, or reward change begins with product meaning. Loyalty identifies known consumers, explains semantics and compatibility concerns, and coordinates tests, but internal consumers release on their own schedules.
21. **Aisha Bello:** That resembles Commerce in change context and impact analysis, but not in external dates, partner eligibility, or production launch authority. Those differences should not be flattened.
22. **Jordan Lee:** Consumers often ask Platform Support what a field means because we are visible. We can route the question and supply platform context, but the domain retains semantic authority.
23. **Lucas Pereira:** We sometimes answer repeated questions in direct chats to keep teams moving. It is locally efficient, yet consumers receive uneven context and the dependency and support cost disappear from records.
24. **Marcus Reed:** A syntactically healthy response may still be semantically wrong for a consumer. That connects to SES-004's outcome-evidence gap without establishing a new indicator.
25. **Lucas Pereira:** A producer deployment does not force consumer adoption. Producers owe discoverable context, impact information, compatibility reasoning, and support; consumers own impact assessment, testing, scheduling, and use within their products.
26. **Daniel Okafor:** Is there a reliable consumer inventory or adoption signal?
27. **Lucas Pereira:** Not demonstrated. I would guess two to four consuming teams can remain on an older interpretation after a typical change, but that is explicitly unverified and may omit indirect consumers.

## 5. Scenario four - Loyalty consumer migration or version transition

28. **Lucas Pereira:** With multiple consumers and release calendars, deployment can finish while migration remains open. Temporary coexistence of behavior or versions sometimes occurs, but we are naming the problem, not designing the solution.
29. **Priya Raman:** Who owns completion: the producer after release, each consumer after adoption, or someone coordinating the portfolio? We have no authority source, so a final RACI would be false precision.
30. **Jordan Lee:** Platform teams can show shared-service status or route dependency questions, but coordination does not authorize consumer deadlines or redefine Loyalty semantics.
31. **Aisha Bello:** Commerce also faces adoption, but external contractual dates and partner readiness change the leverage and consequence. Loyalty's internal relationship must not become the template for partners.
32. **Marcus Reed:** Version coexistence can expand diagnostic ambiguity and recovery paths. We need sampled incidents and change records before calling that a reliability requirement.
33. **Lucas Pereira:** Completion may mean producer deployment, all known consumers migrated, old behavior retired, or the business accepting residual use. Today the meaning appears change-specific and sometimes nobody visibly owns closure.
